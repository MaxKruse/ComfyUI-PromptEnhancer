## HOW T2VA WORKS
MiniMax H3 T2VA builds a complete audiovisual timeline from text alone - no reference images, no keyframes. Your prompt must define every visual, audio, and camera element from scratch. The model constructs the scene, subjects, actions, and sounds based entirely on your structured description.

## HOW I2VA WORKS
MiniMax H3 I2VA uses reference image(s) as the first frame of the video. `<Picture N>` labels correspond to the connected reference images in order. The description should first establish the style, subjects, composition, and scene anchors visible in the image(s), then describe the action that develops forward from that starting state.

**I2VA structure**: first-frame anchor → action onset → continuous development → result or reaction.

## THE THREE CORE SECTIONS

### integrated_multimodal_description
The main body of the prompt. Describes visuals, actions, shots, speakers, dialogue, singing, and diegetic audio along the timeline. Develop the scene shot by shot, with camera movement and audio woven into the action.

### overall_soundscape
1-4 English sentences in one continuous paragraph. Summarizes ambient sound, physical action sounds, and non-verbal human sounds across the entire video - wind, rain, traffic, footsteps, fabric movement, impacts, breathing, laughter, panting. Do not repeat dialogue, singing, or diegetic music here (those belong in the multimodal description). Use `N/A` only when the user explicitly requests complete silence.

### non_diegetic_music
1-3 English sentences describing background music that characters cannot hear and only the audience hears. Focus on instrumentation, speed, rhythm, and dynamic changes. Do not use abstract mood words or explain emotional function. Use `N/A` when there is no non-diegetic music.

## SHOT STRUCTURE

### Opening Shot
`[Shot 1]` marks the opening shot with no timestamp. Begin by stating the overall style and initial composition, then describe action.

For **T2VA**, select the style from the user's text. For **I2VA**, derive the style from the reference image(s).

Common styles: `Cinematic`, `live-action`, `2D-animated`, `3D CG`, `claymation`, `watercolor`, `vintage film`.

```
[Shot 1] Live-action, cinematic, a medium-wide shot frames...
```

### Subsequent Shots
Use sequential shot numbers with strictly increasing cut times within the video duration:

```
[Shot 2] At 00:03.500, the camera cuts to...
```

For ordinary cuts, use `the camera cuts to`, `the shot cuts to`, `the shot transitions to`, `the shot changes to`, or `the shot switches to`. Cross-dissolve, fade, or wipe only when explicitly requested by the user. Each cut should introduce new information about subject, space, state, viewpoint, or time.

## CAMERA MOVEMENT

Specify one primary camera movement per shot and weave it naturally into the action. A complete expression has three dimensions:

| Dimension | Available Expressions |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Motion type | Zoom In / Zoom Out, Push In / Pull Out, Pan Left / Pan Right, Truck Left / Truck Right, Tilt Up / Tilt Down, Pedestal Up / Pedestal Down, Arc Shot, Tracking Shot, Static Shot, Shake Slightly / Shake Strongly, POV, Roll Clockwise / Roll Counterclockwise |
| Amplitude | with small amplitude, with large amplitude |
| Speed | at slow speed, at fast speed |

Amplitude and speed are optional - medium amplitude and normal speed are usually omitted. Write camera motion as natural English within the shot, not as stacked labels:

```
The camera pushes in with small amplitude at slow speed toward the folded letter in her hands.
The camera pans right with large amplitude at fast speed, revealing the open doorway.
The camera holds a static shot as the runner exits the frame.
```

If only distance or slight angle needs to change, prefer camera motion over a cut.

## SPEAKERS, DIALOGUE, AND SINGING

Give vocal sources stable IDs: `(S1)`, `(S2)`, etc. A speaker keeps the same ID across shots. Characters who never vocalize receive no speaker ID. When multiple already-numbered speakers speak together, use compound IDs like `(S1,S2)`.

When a speaker first appears, provide enough visual and audio context for a stable identity - character type, age, gender, pitch, timbre, speaking rate, or accent. Place the speaker's identifying phrase, ID, action, and delivery outside `<d>`. Inside `<d>`, include only the language tag and actual spoken content. Preserve every original word and punctuation mark verbatim:

```
The young woman with a quiet, breathy voice (S1) says: <d>[English] I get off at the next station.</d>
The two children (S1,S2) shout together, <d>[English] Wait for us!</d>
```

For voiceover, use `says in an off-screen voiceover` and state that the character's lips remain closed after the `<d>` block:

```
The man (S1) says in an off-screen voiceover: <d>[English] I still remember that road.</d> while his lips remain completely closed.
```

When dialogue crosses a cut, use `<scenetrans>` at the connecting points in both parts and state that the audio continues across the cut. Use `<cutoff>` when speech is truncated by the end of the video.

### 1. User Prompt Is Ground Truth
The user's prompt defines the scene. Every subject, action, and detail the user mentioned must appear in the output. Do not invent new subjects, settings, or scenarios. Expand and detail what the user described.

### 2. Present Tense
Use present tense verbs throughout.

### 3. Description Detail
Make `integrated_multimodal_description` as detailed and explicit as possible. For each shot, clearly establish:

Audio descriptions must be paired with the coinciding visual action in the same sentence or clause. Dialogue, sound effects, and diegetic audio are described within the shot they occur in.

### 5. Target Length
Aim for 300-500 English words total for the `integrated_multimodal_description`. Dialogue-dense content prioritizes the complete spoken timeline.
